家教的一些反思 November 7, 2026 Reflections on tutoring

我想知道，學生到底卡在哪裡

  • #家教
  • #數學
  • #教學反思

最近教家教時，我一直遇到一個讓我很困惑的問題：同一個觀念教過很多次，也特別出過題目讓學生練習，當下看起來還可以，但下一次在其他題目裡遇到，又會寫錯。

我很想幫忙，可是到現在，我還是沒能釐清，學生到底是哪裡不懂。

好像會了，卻又沒有真的會

分配律已經教過很多次了。我甚至單獨出了一張考卷，讓學生集中練習。那堂課的成果還可以，但好像也只停留在那堂課。之後回到其他題目的計算裡，同樣的錯誤又出現了。

乘法公式也是。我有教，也讓學生背過：

(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2

但實際寫題目時，還是會出現：

(a+b)2=a2+b2(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2(a+b)2=a2+b2

或是：

(a+b)2=2a+2b(a+b)^2=2a+2b(a+b)2=2a+2b

尤其是「平方」和「兩倍」，學生常常搞不清楚。看到這些答案，我會想：是還不理解平方的意思？是公式記混了？還是看到符號時，其實不知道它代表什麼？

我知道答案錯了，卻還不知道，學生是怎麼想到這個答案的。

不只是一個公式的問題

類似的情況，也出現在分數四則運算和根式四則運算。

這些內容我都單獨出過題目讓學生練習，但放回實際解題的過程裡，計算依然會出錯。教排列組合時，加法原理和乘法原理也常常分不清楚。

這讓我越來越困惑。當我把一種題型抽出來，說「我們現在練這個」，學生可以做出一些題目；但當題目沒有先告訴學生該用什麼方法時，好像又不知道該怎麼辦了。

我不確定，當下的練習成果，到底代表理解了多少。也不知道自己是不是太快把「這次做對了」當成「這個觀念已經會了」。

今天的一個算式

今天，學生寫出了：

6(13x−12y)=18x−12y6\left(\frac13x-\frac12y\right)=18x-12y6(31​x−21​y)=18x−12y

正確的結果應該是：

2x−3y2x-3y2x−3y

但看到這個答案時，我更想知道的是：那個 18 和 12，到底是怎麼來的？

學生看到 6 × ⅓ 時，心裡做的是什麼運算？是把乘以三分之一和除以三分之一混在一起了嗎？還是對分數的理解，本來就和我以為的不一樣？

這些都只是我的猜測。我需要聽到學生中間的想法，才有辦法知道問題究竟出在哪裡。

我很想幫忙

這些基礎運算上的困難，也持續影響後面的學習。我很想幫忙，但每次重新講解、重新練習，之後卻又遇到相同的錯誤，我也會覺得挫折，不知道自己還能怎麼教。

對我來說已經很自然的計算，對學生而言，可能每一步都還有不確定的地方。可是當我已經太習慣這些運算時，好像也很容易跳過那些我覺得「應該知道」的事情。

也許我需要先把速度放慢，請學生說說每一步為什麼這樣算。先不要急著把整題重新講完，而是試著找到，我們從哪一步開始有了不同的理解。

我現在還沒有答案。只是很想弄清楚，學生到底卡在哪裡，也想知道，自己可以怎麼教，才能真的幫上忙。

Where Is My Student Getting Stuck?

  • #Tutoring
  • #Mathematics
  • #TeachingReflections

Lately, tutoring has left me with a question I keep coming back to: why does something seem to make sense during practice, only to fall apart when it comes up in another problem? I’ve explained the same ideas many times and prepared exercises specifically for them. In the moment, my student seems to be doing reasonably well. Later, the same mistakes return.

I want to help, but I still haven’t worked out exactly where my student is getting stuck.

It seems to click—until it doesn’t

I’ve gone over the distributive property many times. I even put together a separate worksheet just to practice it. My student did reasonably well that lesson, but the progress seemed to stay within that lesson. When the same operations came up in other problems, the mistakes returned.

The same thing happens with algebraic identities. I’ve explained this one and asked my student to memorize it:

(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a+b)^2=a^2+2ab+b^2(a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2

But when working through a problem, my student still sometimes writes:

(a+b)2=a2+b2(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2(a+b)2=a2+b2

Or:

(a+b)2=2a+2b(a+b)^2=2a+2b(a+b)2=2a+2b

In particular, squaring and doubling often seem to get mixed up. When I see these answers, I wonder: is the meaning of squaring still unclear? Are the formulas getting mixed together? Or do the symbols themselves not yet have a clear meaning?

I can see that the answer is wrong. What I don’t yet understand is how my student arrived at it.

More than one formula

Similar difficulties come up with arithmetic involving fractions and radicals.

I’ve prepared separate exercises for these topics too, but the errors keep appearing when those operations become part of a larger problem. In counting problems, my student also has trouble distinguishing when to use the addition principle and when to use the multiplication principle.

That’s what leaves me so puzzled. When I isolate a type of problem and say, ‘This is what we’re practicing now,’ my student can solve some of the exercises. But when a problem doesn’t come with that cue, it seems much harder to know what to do.

I’m no longer sure how much that immediate success tells me about understanding. I wonder whether I’ve been too quick to take ‘got it right this time’ as ‘understands this idea now.’

One calculation from today

Today, my student wrote:

6(13x−12y)=18x−12y6\left(\frac13x-\frac12y\right)=18x-12y6(31​x−21​y)=18x−12y

The correct result is:

2x−3y2x-3y2x−3y

But what I really wanted to know was: where did the 18 and the 12 come from?

What operation did my student have in mind when looking at six times one third? Was multiplying by one third getting confused with dividing by one third? Or was my student’s understanding of fractions different from what I had assumed?

Those are only guesses. I need to hear the thinking between the steps before I can tell where things went wrong.

I want to help

These difficulties with basic operations keep getting in the way of later learning. I want to help, but it’s frustrating to explain something again, practice it again, and then encounter the same mistakes later. I’m not sure what else to try.

Calculations that feel natural to me may still be full of uncertainty for my student. Being so used to these operations myself, I may be skipping over things I assume are already understood.

Maybe I need to slow down and ask my student to explain why each step makes sense. Instead of rushing to explain the whole problem again, I want to find the point where our understandings begin to differ.

I don’t have an answer yet. I just want to understand where my student is getting stuck—and how I can teach in a way that actually helps.
